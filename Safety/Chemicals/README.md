# Chemical Inventory and SDS Register

Compiled 2026-09-09 from 54 label photographs of the lab's chemical storage. Roughly 50 distinct
products; a few appear twice in the photo set and are listed once here.

**This register is a starting point, not a compliance document.** It records what is on the labels.
It does not record quantities remaining, exact shelf locations, or who owns each container, and
several containers are shared or inherited from other labs (Efimov, Kay). GW EHS holds the
authoritative chemical inventory.

## How to pull an SDS

Every row gives the manufacturer catalogue number and, where it is unambiguous, the CAS number.
Either one retrieves the current SDS:

| Supplier | Where |
|---|---|
| Sigma-Aldrich / Merck / SAFC / Millipore | `sigmaaldrich.com/US/en/search/<catalogue no>` then the SDS tab |
| Thermo / Gibco / Invitrogen | `thermofisher.com` search by REF, or their SDS search page |
| Fisher Chemical / Fisher Science Education | `fishersci.com` SDS search by catalogue number |
| Electron Microscopy Sciences | `emsdiasum.com` product page |
| Ted Pella | `tedpella.com` product page |
| Epredia, RICCA | supplier site, search by REF |

Search by CAS rather than catalogue number when a bottle is old enough that the catalogue number
has been retired. **Always take the SDS from the supplier who made the bottle in your hand**, since
hazard classification and first aid can differ between suppliers for the same substance.

# STOP: three items to resolve before anything else

These came out of the photographs and are listed first because they are time-sensitive. None of
them is a reason to panic; all three are a reason to call GW EHS rather than to tidy up.

## 1. Picric acid, dried out. DO NOT TOUCH THE CAP.

`Aldrich 197378-100G`, CAS 88-89-1, labelled **"EFIMOV OPENED 6/7/19"**.

Picric acid is 2,4,6-trinitrophenol. Wet, it is an ordinary reagent. **Dry, it is a shock- and
friction-sensitive explosive**, and it is supplied "moistened with water" for exactly that reason.
In the photograph there is **yellow crystalline material in the cap threads and around the neck**.

Crystals in the threads are the specific dangerous case, because unscrewing the cap grinds them.

- **Do not open it. Do not move it. Do not attempt to rehydrate it.**
- Leave it where it stands and tell **GW EHS** what it is, that it was opened in 2019, and that
  there is visible crystallisation at the closure. This is a routine call for them; they have a
  procedure and, if needed, a bomb squad relationship.
- Nobody should relocate this bottle to "somewhere safer" first.

## 2. Perchloric acid, roughly ten years old

`Sigma-Aldrich 244252-100ML`, 70%, CAS 7601-90-3, hand-labelled **"Kay 1/22/15"**.

Perchloric acid is a strong oxidiser. Over years it can form metal perchlorates in and around the
closure, and organic contamination plus concentrated perchloric acid is an explosion risk. It also
normally requires a dedicated wash-down fume hood, not a standard one, and it must never be stored
in or near a wooden cabinet or with organics.

Ask EHS to assess it for disposal. Do not open it to check the volume.

## 3. Cyanide salts stored with acids

- `Sigma-Aldrich 207810-25G` **Potassium cyanide**, CAS 151-50-8
- `Sigma-Aldrich 380970-5G` **Sodium cyanide**, CAS 143-33-9
- `Sigma-Aldrich 228710-5G` Sodium nitroferricyanide (nitroprusside), CAS 13755-38-9

Both cyanide labels carry the same sentence: *"Contact with acids liberates very toxic gas."* That
gas is hydrogen cyanide. The same photo set shows hydrochloric, nitric, acetic, glacial acetic,
lactic, glyoxylic and perchloric acids in the same storage.

If cyanides and acids share a cabinet, that is the single highest-value thing to fix, and it is
fixable in an afternoon: cyanides go in their own closed, labelled, acid-free secondary container.
Confirm the lab's cyanide antidote and spill procedure at the same time, and check whether these are
still needed at all. A 25 g bottle of KCN that nobody uses is worth disposing of.

**Also worth flagging:** a propane torch (`IMG_0508`) and a Zippo butane cylinder (`IMG_0489`) are
stored in what appears to be the same cabinet as flammable solvents and oxidisers.

# The posted list is out of date, and that is its own problem

A printed **"Therapeutic Ultrasound Lab, List of Chemicals"** placard for **SEH 5290**, dated
**2/26/2019**, lists 35 products with NFPA health and flammability ratings and Dr Zderic as
emergency contact. A document like this is what an emergency responder reads before entering the
room, so it is worth checking against what is actually on the shelves. This copy is known to be
superseded, and is filed at
[`../reference/posted-list-SEH5290-2019-02-26.jpg`](../reference/posted-list-SEH5290-2019-02-26.jpg);
if a newer revision is posted, redo this comparison against that one.

**None of the five most dangerous things in the photographs appear on it:**

| Missing from the posted list | Why a responder would want to know |
|---|---|
| Picric acid | Shock-sensitive when dry; changes how you enter a fire |
| Perchloric acid | Strong oxidiser; forms shock-sensitive perchlorates |
| Potassium cyanide | Acutely fatal; liberates HCN on contact with acid |
| Sodium cyanide | Acutely fatal; liberates HCN on contact with acid |
| Hydrazine monohydrate | Acutely toxic, flammable, carcinogen |

Also absent: chloroform, acetonitrile, methanol, sodium hydrosulfite (self-heating), sodium
periodate, sodium nitroprusside, 2-mercaptoethanol, carbachol, and every acid in the store except
none, since **no acid at all is on the posted list**: not hydrochloric, nitric, acetic, glacial
acetic, lactic, glyoxylic or perchloric. Nor are the butane and propane cylinders.

The gap runs the other way too. Roughly 27 items on the posted list were not photographed,
including **40% acrylamide/bis-acrylamide**, m-phenylenediamine, bisphenol A diglycidyl ether,
serotonin, 5-hydroxytryptophan, benzalkonium chloride, graphite powder and the Alumilite resins.
Some have probably been used up since 2019. Some are presumably still there.

**Two honest caveats.** The photographs are not certified exhaustive either, so "not photographed"
does not prove "not present". And the picric acid label reads "opened 6/7/19", which is *after* the
placard was printed, so at least one of these arrived after the list was last revised. That is
exactly how a list like this goes stale: it is written once and never revised as bottles arrive.

**What this means practically.** Rebuilding the placard is a bigger job than it looks, because
neither document alone is the inventory: it needs a physical walk of the storage with both lists in
hand. But the five rows above are the ones that matter, and they can be added the same day. GW EHS
holds the authoritative inventory and should be the reconciliation point.

**The 2026 Hazard Information Sheet is a different form.** It is not a 35-row chemical list any
more: it asks for room and building, PI and alternate contacts with 24-hour phone numbers, biosafety
level, an **NFPA diamond**, a **Special Hazards** free-text field, and an access level for
housekeeping. The five items in the STOP section belong in Special Hazards. The 2019 list is
therefore not just stale, it is on a retired template.

## Disposal route, when it comes to that

- **FixIt** at `fixit.assetworks.io/ready` raises the waste pickup. EHS supplies approved labels and
  containers.
- Waste must not sit more than **60 days** from its accumulation start date.
- A chemical waste accumulation area needs the posted sign and a **weekly inspection checklist**
  (containers marked "HAZARDOUS WASTE", accumulation start date recorded, closed, compatible,
  incompatibles separated, spill kit present).
- Anyone handling hazardous waste must have taken **Hazardous Waste Management** training.

**Do not put the picric acid into the normal waste stream.** A suspected dry, shock-sensitive
container is not a FixIt ticket, it is a phone call to EHS on **202-994-4347**, and out of hours
GW Emergency Services on **202-994-6111**.

## Whose chemicals are these

SEH 5290 is shared between **Zderic** (therapeutic ultrasound) and **Kay** (cardiac optical mapping,
absorbance spectroscopy, mitochondrial function, neurocardiology). Hand-marked names on the bottles
include "Kay", "KAY LAB" and "Efimov".

**Most of the frightening reagents look like cardiac physiology, not ultrasound.** This matters
because it changes who needs to be in the conversation, and because nobody should be asked to
dispose of another group's stock without being asked first.

| Reagent | Why a cardiac lab has it |
|---|---|
| **Potassium and sodium cyanide** | Complex IV inhibitor. The standard tool for blocking mitochondrial respiration, which is Kay's stated area |
| **Sodium hydrosulfite (dithionite)** | Oxygen scavenger, used to produce a fully deoxygenated reference spectrum for absorbance spectroscopy |
| **Epinephrine HCl**, **carbachol**, **sodium nitroprusside** | Adrenergic agonist, muscarinic agonist, vasodilator. Perfused-heart pharmacology |
| **L-DOPA**, **glyoxylic acid** | Catecholamine precursor, and glyoxylic acid is the reagent for catecholamine-induced fluorescence histochemistry. Both point at neurocardiology |
| **Picric acid**, **sodium periodate**, **paraformaldehyde** | Bouin's fixative, PLP fixative, immunohistochemistry |

None of that is a criticism of anyone. It is an ordinary consequence of two groups and one cupboard,
and the register is more useful once it says so.

**Efimov is neither current PI.** Igor Efimov ran a cardiac electrophysiology group at GW and has
since moved on. Bottles marked "Efimov", including **the picric acid**, are most likely inherited
from that group. The GW close-out guidelines are explicit that *"items left behind by vacating
Investigators will become the responsibility of the Department"*, so there is an answer to who pays
for disposal, and it is worth raising on the same EHS call rather than letting it stall the removal.

**Practical consequence.** Anything that moves the cyanides, restrains a cylinder, or designates an
area for particularly hazardous substances affects both groups. Those are joint decisions, and Dr
Kay is Associate Dean for Research and Faculty Affairs as well as the co-tenant, so he is worth
including early rather than informing afterwards.

## Secondary containers and orphan labels

Three containers are not manufacturer-labelled and therefore have **no SDS of their own**. Under the
GHS labelling rules a secondary container needs the identity and the hazard on it:

1. **"1N Nitric Acid"**, Pyrex bottle, orange tape. No date, no owner, no hazard pictogram.
2. **"10^-3 M Epinephrine HCl"**, Efimov, dated 8/21. The solution has gone brown, which is what
   oxidised epinephrine looks like, so it is probably past use regardless.
3. **Isopropyl alcohol**, CVS retail bottle marked "KAY LAB".

For these, file the SDS of the **parent substance** and label the container with identity,
concentration, date and owner.
